## Steps

Step 1: Determine the possible atomic normal forms

Take
$$
M=
\lambda f_0\cdots\lambda f_{n-1}
\lambda x_0\cdots\lambda x_{n-1}.
\langle M_0,\ldots,M_{n-1}\rangle
\in\mathcal N_n.
$$
Each $M_j$ has atomic type $p_j$. Since the calculus has no constants, an atomic beta-normal term must ultimately be headed by one of the variables $x_i$.

The only variable of function type whose result is $p_j$ is
$$
f_{j-1}:p_{j-1}\multimap p_j.
$$
Therefore, unless $M_j=x_j$, its outermost form must be
$$
f_{j-1}N
$$
with $N:p_{j-1}$. Applying the same argument repeatedly shows that every component is a directed chain
$$
f_{j-1}\bigl(f_{j-2}(\cdots f_i x_i\cdots)\bigr),
$$
with indices read cyclically. Because every variable is linear, no $f_k$ or $x_k$ can occur twice.

Thus each component consists of one $x_i$ followed by a consecutive interval of the cyclic sequence
$$
f_i,f_{i+1},\ldots,f_{j-1}.
$$

Step 2: Encode every inhabitant by one nonempty subset of the cycle

Call $i$ a start if the component containing $x_i$ applies at least one function to $x_i$. Let
$$
S\subseteq\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z
$$
be the set of starts.

The set $S$ is nonempty because every $f_i$ must be used exactly once. The positive-length chains are disjoint, since the $f_i$ are linear, and together they use all $n$ functions.

List the elements of $S$ in cyclic order. If $s\in S$ and $t$ is the next element of $S$, then the chain beginning at $x_s$ must use exactly
$$
f_s,f_{s+1},\ldots,f_{t-1}
$$
and must occur in tensor component $t$. It cannot stop earlier, because an intervening function would be unused, and it cannot continue farther, because it would overlap the chain beginning at $t$.

For every $i\notin S$, the component $i$ is simply
$$
x_i.
$$

Conversely, any nonempty subset $S$ produces a unique long normal inhabitant by this prescription. Hence
$$
\mathcal N_n
\longleftrightarrow
\{S\subseteq\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z:S\neq\varnothing\}.
$$
This bijection is equivariant for the cyclic action: adding $1$ to all term subscripts sends $S$ to
$$
S+1.
$$

Step 3: Translate the parity condition into the cardinality of the subset

Let the elements of $S$ in cyclic order be
$$
s_1,s_2,\ldots,s_k.
$$
For $i\notin S$, the variable $x_i$ occurs in tensor component $i$, so the induced permutation fixes $i$.

For the starts,
$$
x_{s_1},x_{s_2},\ldots,x_{s_k}
$$
occur respectively in components
$$
s_2,s_3,\ldots,s_k,s_1.
$$
Thus the induced permutation is one $k$-cycle on $S$ and fixes its complement. Its sign is therefore
$$
(-1)^{k-1}.
$$
Consequently the permutation is odd exactly when
$$
|S|
$$
is even.

The required terms therefore correspond exactly to nonempty even-cardinality subsets whose cyclic orbit has size $n$.

Step 4: Characterize the subsets with a non-full cyclic orbit

Since
$$
n=2^r,
$$
every proper divisor of $n$ divides $n/2$.

Suppose a subset $S$ is fixed by a nonzero cyclic shift $k$. Put
$$
d=\gcd(n,k).
$$
The subgroup generated by the shift $k$ is the same as the subgroup generated by the shift $d$, so $S$ is periodic with period $d$. Since $d<n$ and $n$ is a power of $2$,
$$
d\mid\frac n2.
$$
Hence $S$ is fixed by the half-turn
$$
i\longmapsto i+\frac n2.
$$

The converse is immediate: a subset fixed by the half-turn has orbit size at most $n/2$.

Therefore a subset has cyclic orbit smaller than $n$ if and only if it is fixed by the half-turn.

Step 5: Count the full orbits

Exactly half of the $2^n$ subsets of an $n$-element set have even cardinality, so there are
$$
2^{n-1}
$$
even subsets.

A subset fixed by the half-turn is determined independently on the $n/2$ pairs
$$
\left\{i,i+\frac n2\right\}.
$$
Hence there are
$$
2^{n/2}
$$
such subsets. Every one has even cardinality because its elements occur in pairs.

Both counts include the empty set. Since the empty set is half-turn invariant, removing it from both sides does not change their difference. Thus the number of admissible terms whose cyclic orbit has full size is
$$
2^{n-1}-2^{n/2}.
$$

Every such orbit contains exactly $n$ terms, and the parity condition is constant on an orbit because cyclic relabeling conjugates the induced permutation. Therefore the number of required orbits is
$$
\frac{2^{n-1}-2^{n/2}}{n}.
$$
Substituting $n=2^r$ gives
$$
\frac{2^{2^r-1}-2^{2^{r-1}}}{2^r}.
$$

Final Answer: $\boxed{\frac{2^{2^r-1}-2^{2^{r-1}}}{2^r}}$

## Answer

$\frac{2^{2^r-1}-2^{2^{r-1}}}{2^r}$
