## Steps

Step 1: Convert common quadratic subfields into a binary bicycle space
Let
$$
C_1=\mathbb F_2^{E(G_r)}.
$$
Because the primes $q_e$ are distinct, their square classes are linearly independent. For any subspace $U\subseteq C_1$, the multiquadratic field generated by square roots of the products represented by $U$ has sign-change Galois group dual to $U$; its index-two fixed fields are exactly
$$
\mathbb Q\left(\sqrt{\prod_e q_e^{c_e}}\right),
\qquad
0\neq(c_e)\in U.
$$
The vectors of the $a_v$ are vertex stars, so $K_r$ corresponds to the cut space
$$
A=\operatorname{span}\{\delta(v):v\in V\}.
$$
The vectors of the $b_T$ are triangle boundaries, so $L_r$ corresponds to the triangle space $B$.

We claim $B$ is the full cycle space $Z$. A weight-two edge, together with the two weight-one edges through an intermediate vertex, is a triangle. A weight-three edge similarly reduces modulo one triangle to a weight-one edge plus a weight-two edge. Hence every cycle is congruent modulo $B$ to a cycle in the ordinary $r$-cube. A closed cube walk is a word in coordinate flips in which each coordinate occurs an even number of times. Swapping two adjacent unequal flips changes the walk by one square, so repeated swaps bring equal flips together, where they cancel as backtracks. Thus cube cycles are generated by squares. Each square is the sum of two triangles of $G_r$ using its weight-two diagonal, so
$$
B=Z.
$$

Define
$$
\delta:\mathbb F_2^V\to C_1,\qquad
(\delta f)(\{u,v\})=f(u)+f(v).
$$
Since $G_r$ is connected, $\ker\delta$ is the one-dimensional space of constant functions. If $\partial:C_1\to\mathbb F_2^V$ is the vertex-edge incidence map, then $Z=\ker\partial$, hence
$$
A\cap B=A\cap Z=\delta(\ker L),
\qquad
L=\partial\delta.
$$
If
$$
D=\dim_{\mathbb F_2}\ker L,
$$
the common square-class space therefore has dimension $D-1$.

Step 2: Identify the binary Laplacian with multiplication by a cubic element
Identify functions on $V$ with
$$
R=\mathbb F_2[x_1,\ldots,x_r]/(x_1^2-1,\ldots,x_r^2-1),
$$
where multiplication by $x^s$ translates by $s$. Put $y_i=x_i+1$, so $y_i^2=0$, and let $\sigma_j(z)$ denote the elementary symmetric polynomial of degree $j$ in variables $z_1,\ldots,z_r$. The graph degree is
$$
N=\binom r1+\binom r2+\binom r3,
$$
so $L$ is multiplication by
$$
\theta=N+\sigma_1(x)+\sigma_2(x)+\sigma_3(x).
$$
For a fixed monomial $y_T$ of degree $j\leq k$, its coefficient in $\sigma_k(x)$ is
$$
\binom{r-j}{k-j},
$$
because $x_i=1+y_i$ and the other $k-j$ indices are chosen outside $T$. Hence the constant coefficient of $\theta$ is $N+N=0$, while the coefficients of $\sigma_1(y)$ and $\sigma_2(y)$ are
$$
1+(r-1)+\binom{r-1}{2}
$$
and
$$
1+(r-2).
$$
When $r\equiv3\pmod4$, $r-1\equiv2\pmod4$, so $\binom{r-1}{2}$ is odd; both displayed coefficients therefore vanish in $\mathbb F_2$. The cubic coefficient is $1$, giving
$$
\theta=\sigma_3(y).
$$
Moreover, $y_i^2=0$, and each cubic monomial occurs three times in $\sigma_1(y)\sigma_2(y)$, so
$$
\sigma_3(y)=\sigma_1(y)\sigma_2(y).
$$
Thus $D$ is the nullity of multiplication by $\sigma_3(y)$ on $R$.

Step 3: Compute the nullity by a symplectic tensor decomposition
Write $r=2m+1$. The quadratic element $\sigma_2(y)$ has alternating coefficient matrix with zeros on the diagonal and ones off the diagonal. For $c=(c_i)$ and $S=\sum_i c_i$, this matrix sends $c$ to the vector with coordinates $S+c_i$. Its kernel is therefore exactly the line spanned by
$$
t=\sigma_1(y).
$$
The remaining rank is $2m$. Starting with any $p$ outside this radical, choose $q$ paired to $p$ with value $1$; replacing each remaining vector $v$ by
$$
v+B(v,q)p+B(v,p)q
$$
makes it orthogonal to $p$ and $q$. Repeating gives a basis
$$
t,p_1,q_1,\ldots,p_m,q_m
$$
in which
$$
\sigma_2(y)=\omega=\sum_{i=1}^m p_iq_i.
$$
Therefore
$$
\sigma_3(y)=t\omega.
$$
Since every linear form squares to zero, the algebra is
$$
R\cong\Lambda(t)\otimes\Lambda(W),
\qquad
W=\operatorname{span}\{p_i,q_i:1\leq i\leq m\}.
$$
Multiplication by $t\omega$ has the same rank as
$$
d:\Lambda(W)\to\Lambda(W),\qquad d(a)=\omega a.
$$

Now
$$
\Lambda(W)=\bigotimes_{i=1}^m\Lambda(p_i,q_i),
$$
and $d$ is the sum of the maps multiplying the $i$-th factor by $p_iq_i$. On one factor,
$$
1\longmapsto p_iq_i,
\qquad
p_i\longmapsto0,
\qquad
q_i\longmapsto0,
\qquad
p_iq_i\longmapsto0.
$$
Thus it splits as the two-dimensional pair $\langle1,p_iq_i\rangle$ and the zero-differential space $\langle p_i,q_i\rangle$. On any tensor summand containing such a pair, define $h(p_iq_i)=1$ and $h(1)=0$ on the first pair factor. Then $dh+hd$ is the identity there; all terms from the other factors occur twice and cancel in characteristic two. Hence only
$$
\bigotimes_{i=1}^m\langle p_i,q_i\rangle
$$
contributes to $\ker d/\operatorname{im}d$, giving dimension $2^m$. Since $d^2=0$, if $\rho=\operatorname{rank}d$, then
$$
2^m=(4^m-\rho)-\rho.
$$
Consequently
$$
\rho=2^{2m-1}-2^{m-1}.
$$
The ambient algebra $R$ has dimension $2^{2m+1}$, so
$$
D=2^{2m+1}-\rho
=3\cdot2^{2m-1}+2^{m-1}
=3\cdot2^{r-2}+2^{(r-3)/2}.
$$

Step 4: Impose the two ramification coordinates and count the fields
For $f\in\ker L$, set
$$
\alpha(f)=(\delta f)(\varepsilon_1),
\qquad
\beta(f)=(\delta f)(\varepsilon_3).
$$
These functionals are independent on $\ker L$. First take
$$
f_1=\sigma_1(y).
$$
Because $r$ is odd,
$$
f_1=1+\sum_{i=1}^r x_i,
$$
so its coefficients at $0,e_1,e_1+e_2+e_3$ are $1,1,0$, giving
$$
(\alpha(f_1),\beta(f_1))=(0,1).
$$
Next take
$$
f_2=\prod_{i\neq1}y_i=\prod_{i\neq1}(1+x_i).
$$
Its coefficient is $1$ exactly for subsets not containing $1$, so
$$
(\alpha(f_2),\beta(f_2))=(1,1).
$$
Both belong to $\ker L$: using $\sigma_3=\sigma_1\sigma_2$ and $\sigma_1^2=0$ gives $\sigma_3f_1=0$, while every cubic monomial in $\sigma_3$ repeats a variable when multiplied by $f_2$. Hence
$$
(\alpha,\beta):\ker L\to\mathbb F_2^2
$$
is surjective, and its fiber over $(1,1)$ has $2^{D-2}$ elements.

The constant function is
$$
\sum_{v\in V}x^v=\prod_{i=1}^r(1+x_i)=\prod_{i=1}^r y_i.
$$
It spans $\ker\delta$ and has $\alpha=\beta=0$. Thus each cut in the target fiber has exactly two preimages, and the number of common square classes with both specified coordinates equal to $1$ is
$$
R_r=2^{D-3}.
$$
For a squarefree product of the odd primes $q_e$, the prime $q_e$ ramifies in its quadratic field exactly when the $e$-coordinate is $1$. Substituting the value of $D$ gives
$$
R_r=2^{3\cdot2^{r-2}+2^{(r-3)/2}-3}.
$$

Final Answer: $\boxed{2^{3\cdot2^{r-2}+2^{(r-3)/2}-3}}$
