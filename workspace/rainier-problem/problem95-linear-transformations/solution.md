## Steps

Step 1: Convert the invariant Lagrangian subspaces into a norm equation in a cyclic algebra

Let
$$
E=\operatorname{span}\{e_i:i\in\mathbb Z/\ell\mathbb Z\},
\qquad
W=\mathbb F_p^{\ell},
$$
and identify both $E$ and $F$ with $W$ by the displayed bases. Since $L\cap F=\{0\}$ and $\dim L=\dim E=\ell$, projection onto $E$ restricts to an isomorphism on $L$. Hence there is a unique linear map $A:W\to W$ such that
$$
L=\{(x,Ax):x\in W\}.
$$
For the standard dot product on $W$,
$$
\omega((x,u),(y,v))=x\cdot v-y\cdot u,
$$
so this graph is isotropic exactly when $A$ is symmetric.

Let $C$ be the cyclic shift and let $G$ be the permutation $Ge_i=e_{gi}$. Since $T$ and $S$ are invertible, the inclusions $T(L)\subseteq L$ and $S(L)\subseteq L$ are equalities. The two invariance conditions are respectively
$$
AC=CA,
\qquad
AGA=G.
$$
Identify $W$ with
$$
R=\mathbb F_p[X]/(X^\ell-1)
$$
by $e_i\leftrightarrow X^i$. Since $1,X,\ldots,X^{\ell-1}$ is the orbit of $1$ under multiplication by $X$, every endomorphism commuting with $C$ is multiplication by a unique $a\in R$. Define
$$
\gamma(u)(X)=u(X^g).
$$
Then $G$ acts as $\gamma$, and
$$
m_aGm_a=m_{a\gamma(a)}G.
$$
Thus $AGA=G$ is equivalent to
$$
a\gamma(a)=1.
$$
This equation gives $\gamma(a)=a^{-1}$ and hence $\gamma^2(a)=a$. Because $g^2\equiv-1\pmod\ell$,
$$
\gamma^2(a)(X)=a(X^{-1}).
$$
The transpose of multiplication by $a$ in the basis $1,X,\ldots,X^{\ell-1}$ is multiplication by $a(X^{-1})$, so $m_a$ is symmetric. Therefore the isotropy condition is automatic once the norm equation holds, and the required subspaces are in bijection with
$$
\{a\in R^\times:a\gamma(a)=1\}.
$$
Finally, $D$ is the graph of the identity, so
$$
d(L)=\dim_{\mathbb F_p}\ker(m_a-I).
$$

Step 2: Decompose the cyclic algebra by Frobenius orbits and express the intersection dimension componentwise

Choose a primitive $\ell$th root of unity $\xi$ in an algebraic closure of $\mathbb F_p$. Put
$$
r=\operatorname{ord}_{\ell}(p),
\qquad
H=\langle p\rangle\leq(\mathbb Z/\ell\mathbb Z)^\times.
$$
The Frobenius orbit of $\xi$ is
$$
\xi,\xi^p,\ldots,\xi^{p^{r-1}},
$$
and it first repeats after $r$ terms. The polynomial
$$
\prod_{j=0}^{r-1}\left(Y-\xi^{p^j}\right)
$$
is Frobenius-fixed and therefore lies in $\mathbb F_p[Y]$. Any polynomial in $\mathbb F_p[Y]$ that vanishes at $\xi$ also vanishes at all these $r$ distinct conjugates, so its degree is at least $r$. Hence the displayed degree-$r$ polynomial is the minimal polynomial of $\xi$, and
$$
K=\mathbb F_p(\xi)
$$
has $p^r$ elements. The same field is generated by every primitive root $\xi^s$ because $s$ is invertible modulo $\ell$.

Let the cosets of $H$ be represented by $s$. Evaluation gives an $\mathbb F_p$-algebra map
$$
R\longrightarrow \mathbb F_p\times\prod_{sH}K,
\qquad
u\longmapsto\left(u(1),(u(\xi^s))_{sH}\right).
$$
If a representative of degree less than $\ell$ maps to zero, then it vanishes at $1$ and, by Frobenius, at every $\xi^{sp^j}$. These are all $\ell$ roots of $X^\ell-1$, so the representative is zero. The map is therefore injective. Both sides have dimension
$$
1+\frac{\ell-1}{r}\,r=\ell,
$$
so it is an isomorphism.

On the component indexed by $sH$,
$$
\gamma(u)(\xi^s)=u(\xi^{sg}).
$$
Moreover multiplication by $a-1$ is zero on a field component exactly when the corresponding value of $a$ equals $1$, and otherwise it is invertible there. Consequently
$$
d(L)=[a(1)=1]+r\,\#\{sH:a(\xi^s)=1\}.
$$

Step 3: Determine the orbit size of multiplication by $g$ on the Frobenius factors

Put
$$
c=\gcd(4,r),
\qquad
h=\frac{4}{c}.
$$
The coset $gH$ has order exactly $h$.

If $r$ is odd, $H$ has odd order, so $-1\notin H$. Since $g^2=-1$, the order of $gH$ is $4$.

If $r\equiv2\pmod4$, the element $p^{r/2}$ has order $2$ modulo $\ell$. The only nontrivial solution of $x^2=1$ modulo the prime $\ell$ is $-1$, so
$$
p^{r/2}\equiv-1\pmod\ell.
$$
Thus $g^2\in H$, while $g\notin H$ because the cyclic group $H=\langle p\rangle$ has no element of order $4$. Hence $gH$ has order $2$.

If $4\mid r$, the same argument gives $p^{r/2}\equiv-1\pmod\ell$, so
$$
\left(p^{r/4}\right)^2\equiv-1\pmod\ell.
$$
The two roots of $x^2\equiv-1\pmod\ell$ are $g$ and $-g$. Thus $p^{r/4}=\pm g$ modulo $\ell$. Since both $p^{r/4}$ and $-1$ lie in $H$, either sign implies $g\in H$, so $gH$ has order $1$.

Multiplication by $g$ therefore splits the $(\ell-1)/r$ nontrivial field factors into orbits of size $h$, so the number of such orbits is
$$
N=\frac{\ell-1}{rh}=\frac{c(\ell-1)}{4r}.
$$
At the factor $X=1$, the norm equation is $a(1)^2=1$. Since $p$ is odd, the two choices are $1$ and $-1$, contributing the factor
$$
1+z.
$$

Step 4: Count the norm-one choices on one orbit, including the weight from fixed vectors

First suppose $r$ is odd, so $h=4$. On one orbit choose representatives
$$
s,sg,sg^2,sg^3
$$
and write the corresponding values of $a$ as $u_0,u_1,u_2,u_3\in K^\times$. The norm equation gives
$$
u_0u_1=u_1u_2=u_2u_3=u_3u_0=1.
$$
Hence
$$
(u_0,u_1,u_2,u_3)=(u,u^{-1},u,u^{-1})
$$
with arbitrary $u\in K^\times$. There are $p^r-1$ choices. Exactly one has all four components equal to $1$, and it contributes $4r$ to $d(L)$. Thus one orbit contributes
$$
p^r-2+z^{4r}.
$$

Next suppose $r\equiv2\pmod4$, so $h=2$. For an orbit write
$$
u=a(\xi^s),
\qquad
v=a(\xi^{sg}).
$$
Because $g^2\equiv-1\equiv p^{r/2}\pmod\ell$, the two component equations are
$$
uv=1,
\qquad
v\,u^{p^{r/2}}=1.
$$
Thus $v=u^{-1}$ and $u^{p^{r/2}}=u$. Every root of $Y^{p^{r/2}}-Y$ also satisfies $Y^{p^r}-Y=0$, and the derivative of the former polynomial is $-1$. Over an algebraic closure it therefore has exactly $p^{r/2}$ distinct roots, all lying in $K$. There are $p^{r/2}-1$ nonzero choices for $u$. One gives both components equal to $1$, so the orbit contribution is
$$
p^{r/2}-2+z^{2r}.
$$

Finally suppose $4\mid r$, so $h=1$. Write $g\equiv p^k\pmod\ell$. The equation on one field factor is
$$
u\,u^{p^k}=1.
$$
Applying the $p^k$ Frobenius and comparing gives $u^{p^{2k}}=u$. Since
$$
p^{2k}\equiv g^2\equiv-1\equiv p^{r/2}\pmod\ell,
$$
the order of $p$ modulo $\ell$ gives
$$
2k\equiv\frac r2\pmod r.
$$
Hence $u^{p^{r/2}}=u$. The fixed set
$$
K_0=\{x\in K:x^{p^{r/2}}=x\}
$$
has exactly $p^{r/2}$ elements by the same polynomial-root argument, and it is a field because the Frobenius map preserves sums, products, and inverses. Put $Q=p^{r/4}$, so $|K_0|=Q^2$. From $2k\equiv r/2\pmod r$ we get $k\equiv r/4\pmod{r/2}$, so on $K_0$ the original equation becomes
$$
u^{Q+1}=1.
$$
The polynomial $Y^{Q+1}-1$ divides $Y^{Q^2-1}-1$, which splits over $K_0$ into all nonzero elements of $K_0$. Its derivative is $(Q+1)Y^Q\neq0$, so it has exactly $Q+1$ roots. One is $1$ and contributes $r$ to $d(L)$; the other $Q=p^{r/4}$ choices contribute zero. Thus one orbit contributes
$$
p^{r/4}+z^r.
$$

Step 5: Assemble the orbit factors and write the result in a single formula

For $c=\gcd(4,r)$, the three orbit contributions from Step 4 are unified by
$$
p^{r/c}-2+2[4\mid r]+z^{4r/c}.
$$
There are
$$
\frac{c(\ell-1)}{4r}
$$
independent nontrivial orbits, and Step 3 gives the additional factor $1+z$ from $X=1$. Therefore
$$
P_{p,\ell}(z)
=
(1+z)
\left(
p^{r/\gcd(4,r)}-2+2[4\mid r]+z^{4r/\gcd(4,r)}
\right)^{\gcd(4,r)(\ell-1)/(4r)}.
$$
The highest power is $z^\ell$ with coefficient $1$, corresponding to $a=1$ and hence $L=D$, which checks the full dimension contribution. The formula depends only on $p$ and $\ell$, so the two possible choices of $g$ give the same polynomial.

Final Answer: $\boxed{(1+z)\left(p^{r/\gcd(4,r)}-2+2[4\mid r]+z^{4r/\gcd(4,r)}\right)^{\gcd(4,r)(\ell-1)/(4r)}}$

---

## Answer

$(1+z)\left(p^{r/\gcd(4,r)}-2+2[4\mid r]+z^{4r/\gcd(4,r)}\right)^{\gcd(4,r)(\ell-1)/(4r)}$
