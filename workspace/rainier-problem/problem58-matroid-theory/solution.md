## Steps

Step 1: Reduce the marked conic to an eight-point subset of the projective line

In the basis $U^2,UV,V^2$ of $W_p$, the map
$$
x\longmapsto [\ell_x^2]
$$
is the quadratic Veronese parametrization of a nonsingular conic. The second
symmetric power sends every element of $\operatorname{PGL}_2(\mathbb F_p)$
to a projectivity preserving this conic. Let $\mathcal C$ be the geometric
conic containing $\mathcal C_p$. If $g$ permutes $\mathcal C_p$, then the
conics $\mathcal C$ and $g(\mathcal C)$ share $p+1\geq12$ points. Distinct
plane conics meet in at most four points, so $g(\mathcal C)=\mathcal C$.
Restriction to $\mathcal C$ identifies its projective automorphisms with
fractional-linear maps. Therefore
$$
G_{p,a}\cong\operatorname{Stab}_{\operatorname{PGL}_2(\mathbb F_p)}(S_a),
\qquad
S_a=\{\infty,0,1,-1,a,-a,a^{-1},-a^{-1}\}.
$$

The four maps
$$
V=\left\{x,\ -x,\ \frac1x,\ -\frac1x\right\}
$$
always stabilize $S_a$. Because $a\in\mathcal A_p$, the $V$-orbits on
$S_a$ have sizes
$$
\{0,\infty\},\qquad \{1,-1\},\qquad
\{a,-a,a^{-1},-a^{-1}\};
$$
thus their size pattern is $2+2+4$.

Step 2: Restrict every possible larger stabilizer by its orbit sizes

Let $H$ be a finite subgroup of $\operatorname{PGL}_2(\overline{\mathbb F}_p)$
whose order is prime to $p$. Every nonidentity element of $H$ has exactly two
fixed points. Moreover, a point stabilizer is cyclic: after moving the point
to $\infty$, its elements are affine maps $x\mapsto ux+v$; the kernel of the
map to $u$ is a $p$-group, hence is trivial, and the image is a finite subgroup
of $\overline{\mathbb F}_p^{\,*}$.

Let $e_1,\ldots,e_r$ be the orders of the stabilizers on the exceptional
orbits. Counting pairs $(g,x)$ with $g\neq1$ and $g(x)=x$ in two ways gives
$$
2(|H|-1)
=\sum_{i=1}^r\frac{|H|}{e_i}(e_i-1),
$$
or
$$
\sum_{i=1}^r\left(1-\frac1{e_i}\right)
=2-\frac2{|H|}.
$$
Since every summand is at least $1/2$, one has $r\leq3$. The equation rules
out $r=1$; for $r=2$ it forces $e_1=e_2=|H|$, so $H$ is cyclic. If $r=3$
and $2\leq e_1\leq e_2\leq e_3$, the inequality
$e_1^{-1}+e_2^{-1}+e_3^{-1}>1$ leaves only the following rows:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
(e_1,e_2,e_3)&|H|&\text{exceptional orbit sizes}\\
(2,2,n)&2n&(n,n,2)\\
(2,3,3)&12&(6,4,4)\\
(2,3,4)&24&(12,8,6)\\
(2,3,5)&60&(30,20,12)
\end{array}
$$
Every orbit not displayed in the last column has trivial stabilizer and hence
has size $|H|$. We now invoke the standard tame finite-subgroup classification:
over an algebraically closed field with $p\nmid|H|$, a finite subgroup of
$\operatorname{PGL}_2$ is cyclic, dihedral, tetrahedral
$A_4$, octahedral $S_4$, or icosahedral $A_5$. Their three-point signatures
are respectively $(2,2,n),(2,3,3),(2,3,4),(2,3,5)$ in the noncyclic cases.
Thus the four rows above are exhaustive, with the stated geometric names.

This applies to $G_{p,a}$ because it acts faithfully on eight points, so its
order divides $8!$ and is prime to $p\geq11$. A cyclic group cannot contain
$V$. We now dispose of the two polyhedral rows explicitly.

For $(2,3,3)$, the exceptional orbit sizes are $6,4,4$, while every other
orbit has size $12$. Thus an invariant set of size $8$ can only be the union
of the two size-$4$ orbits. If $x$ lies in either one, then $|H_x|=3$, so
$$
V\cap H_x=1,
\qquad
|Vx|=\frac{|V|}{|V\cap H_x|}=4.
$$
Each exceptional four-point orbit is therefore one $V$-orbit, and the induced
$V$-pattern is $4+4$, not the required $2+2+4$. For $(2,3,4)$, the orbit
sizes are $12,8,6$ and $24$, so the unique way to obtain eight points is the
exceptional size-$8$ orbit. Here again $|H_x|=24/8=3$, whence $V\cap H_x=1$;
the orbit splits into two $V$-orbits of size $4$. The row $(2,3,5)$ has no
orbit of size at most $8$. This proves that neither the tetrahedral nor the
octahedral case can realize the orbit pattern of $S_a$.

It remains to inspect the dihedral row $(n,n,2)$. Since it contains $V$, the
integer $n$ is even. For a group larger than $V$, its orbit sizes can total
$8$ only when $n=4,6,8$. If $n=4$, a generic size-$8$ orbit splits into two
$V$-orbits of size $4$. The only other possibility is the union of the two
special size-$4$ orbits. Conjugate the action over $\overline{\mathbb F}_p$
so that it is generated by
$$
r(x)=ix,\qquad s(x)=\frac1x,\qquad i^2=-1.
$$
Its two special four-point orbits are
$$
O_+=\{x:x^4=1\}=\mu_4,
\qquad
O_-=\{x:x^4=-1\}=\eta\mu_4,
$$
where $\eta^4=-1$. Hence
$$
O_+\cup O_-=\{x:x^8=1\}=\mu_8.
$$
The maps $q(x)=\eta x$ and $s(x)=1/x$ satisfy $q^8=s^2=1$ and
$sqs=q^{-1}$; the maps $q^j,sq^j$ are distinct for $0\leq j<8$. They
therefore give a dihedral stabilizer of order $16$. It is the full stabilizer:
the polyhedral cases were excluded above, and a larger dihedral group has no
orbit union of total size $8$. After undoing the conjugation, $q$ is defined
over $\mathbb F_p$, because it permutes $S_a$ and a fractional-linear map
taking three rational points to rational points has coefficients in
$\mathbb F_p$ up to scalar. Thus the full stabilizer of the two special
four-point orbits has order exactly $16$.

For $n=6$, the only sum $8$ is the exceptional pattern $2+6$, producing
order $12$. For $n=8$, the only possibility is one exceptional orbit of size
$8$, producing order $16$. Consequently a full stabilizer larger than $V$
has exactly one of the following two forms:
$$
\text{order }12\text{ with orbit pattern }2+6,
\qquad
\text{order }16\text{ with one orbit of size }8.
$$

Step 3: Determine the parameters producing the order-$12$ configuration

In the order-$12$ case, the unique two-point orbit is preserved by $V$, so it
must be either $\{0,\infty\}$ or $\{1,-1\}$.

If the two-point orbit is $\{0,\infty\}$, the other six points form a scaled
copy of the sixth roots of unity. Since this orbit contains $1$, the scale is
itself a sixth root, and therefore
$$
\{1,-1,a,-a,a^{-1},-a^{-1}\}=\mu_6,
$$
where $\mu_6$ denotes the sixth roots of unity. This is equivalent to
$$
a^4+a^2+1=0.
$$

If the two-point orbit is $\{1,-1\}$, apply
$$
\phi(x)=\frac{x-1}{x+1},
$$
which sends $\{1,-1\}$ to $\{0,\infty\}$ and sends $\{0,\infty\}$ to
$\{-1,1\}$. With
$$
y=\phi(a)=\frac{a-1}{a+1},
$$
the remaining four images are $\{y,-y,y^{-1},-y^{-1}\}$. They complete
$\{1,-1\}$ to $\mu_6$ exactly when $y^4+y^2+1=0$. Clearing the denominator
gives
$$
(a-1)^4+(a-1)^2(a+1)^2+(a+1)^4
=3a^4+10a^2+3,
$$
and
$$
3a^4+10a^2+3=(a^2+3)(3a^2+1).
$$
Thus the complete order-$12$ candidate locus is
$$
(a^4+a^2+1)(a^2+3)(3a^2+1)=0.
$$
Conversely, either condition turns $S_a$, directly or through $\phi$, into
$\{0,\infty\}\cup\mu_6$, whose rotations and inversion give a dihedral
subgroup of order $12$; its fullness is settled after the loci are separated.

Step 4: Determine the parameters producing the order-$16$ configuration

Write the dihedral action as
$$
\langle r,s:r^8=s^2=1,\ srs=r^{-1}\rangle
$$
on the vertices numbered modulo $8$. A Klein four subgroup with orbit pattern
$2+2+4$ is generated by $r^4$ and a reflection through two opposite vertices.
After shifting the vertex labels, its three orbits are
$$
\{0,4\},\qquad\{2,6\},\qquad\{1,3,5,7\}.
$$
Matching these with the three $V$-orbits, reversing the cycle, and replacing
$a$ by one of $\pm a^{\pm1}$ allows the cyclic order
$$
0,\ a,\ 1,\ a^{-1},\ \infty,\ -a^{-1},\ -1,\ -a.
$$
Interchanging the two size-$2$ orbits causes no new condition: the map
$x\mapsto(x+1)/(x-1)$ performs that interchange, and substitution transforms
$a^4-6a^2+1$ into
$$
-\frac{4(a^4-6a^2+1)}{(a-1)^4}.
$$

Let $r$ be the rotation sending the first four displayed entries to their
successors. Writing
$$
r(x)=\frac{\alpha x+a}{\gamma x+1},
$$
the equations $r(a)=1$ and $r(1)=a^{-1}$ give
$$
\gamma=-a,\qquad \alpha=-a-1+a^{-1}.
$$
The next required image is $r(\infty)=-a^{-1}$, and hence
$$
\frac{-a-1+a^{-1}}{-a}=-a^{-1}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
a^2+2a-1=0.
$$
Reversing signs gives the second possibility $a^2-2a-1=0$. Their product is
$$
(a^2+2a-1)(a^2-2a-1)=a^4-6a^2+1.
$$

Conversely, if $a^2+2a-1=0$, then
$$
r(x)=\frac{x+a}{1-ax}
$$
cycles the eight displayed points in that order, while $x\mapsto1/x$ reverses
the cycle. These maps generate a dihedral group of order $16$. If
$a^2-2a-1=0$, replace $a$ by $-a$; this does not change $S_a$. Fullness is
again settled after disjointness.

Step 5: Separate the two loci and finish the classification

Put $x=a^2$. The three order-$12$ factors are
$$
x^2+x+1,\qquad x+3,\qquad 3x+1,
$$
whereas the order-$16$ equation is $x^2-6x+1=0$. A common root of the first
and fourth equations would satisfy $7x=0$. Substitution of $x=-3$ or
$x=-1/3$ into the fourth equation gives $28$ or $28/9$. Thus the two loci are
disjoint for $p\geq11$. The three order-$12$ factors are also pairwise
disjoint: substituting $x=-3$ or $x=-1/3$ into $x^2+x+1$ gives $7$ or $7/9$,
and $-3=-1/3$ would force $8=0$.

At $x=0,1,-1$, the order-$12$ product has respective values $3,48,-4$, while
$x^2-6x+1$ has respective values $1,-4,8$. These are nonzero for $p\geq11$,
so every listed root is admissible. The orbit classification permits no full
stabilizer larger than $V$ except the order-$12$ and order-$16$ cases already
constructed. Since their loci are disjoint, the first construction has full
stabilizer of order $12$, while the second has full stabilizer of order $16$.
Every admissible parameter outside both loci therefore has $G_{p,a}=V$ and
$|G_{p,a}|=4$.

Final Answer: $\boxed{(\{a:(a^4+a^2+1)(a^2+3)(3a^2+1)=0\},\{a:a^4-6a^2+1=0\})}$
