## Steps

Step 1: Convert the dilation equations into two factored shift equations

Put
$$
g(t)=f(e^t),\qquad A(t)=P(g(t)),
$$
and define
$$
H(s)=e^{-2s}A(e^s),\qquad s\in\mathbb{R}.
$$
Let
$$
a=\log2,\qquad b=\log3.
$$

Since
$$
H(s+ka)=\frac{A(2^ke^s)}{4^ke^{2s}},
$$
the first given identity becomes
$$
H(s+4a)-6H(s+3a)+13H(s+2a)-12H(s+a)+4H(s)=0.
$$
Its shift polynomial factors as
$$
z^4-6z^3+13z^2-12z+4=(z-1)^2(z-2)^2.
$$

Likewise, the second identity becomes
$$
H(s+4b)-8H(s+3b)+22H(s+2b)-24H(s+b)+9H(s)=0,
$$
with
$$
z^4-8z^3+22z^2-24z+9=(z-1)^2(z-3)^2.
$$

If $E_h$ denotes the shift
$$
(E_hF)(s)=F(s+h),
$$
then
$$
(E_a-1)^2(E_a-2)^2H=0,
$$
$$
(E_b-1)^2(E_b-3)^2H=0.
$$

Step 2: Split the first recurrence into its two generalized modes

The polynomial identity
$$
1=(5-2z)(z-1)^2+(2z-1)(z-2)^2
$$
gives a decomposition
$$
H=U+V,
$$
where
$$
U=(2E_a-1)(E_a-2)^2H
$$
and
$$
V=(5-2E_a)(E_a-1)^2H.
$$
Because the shift operators commute,
$$
(E_a-1)^2U=0,
$$
$$
(E_a-2)^2V=0.
$$
The same commutativity shows that both $U$ and $V$ still satisfy
$$
(E_b-1)^2(E_b-3)^2F=0.
$$

Step 3: Show that the unit-eigenvalue component is affine

Let
$$
R=(E_a-1)U.
$$
Since $(E_a-1)^2U=0$,
$$
R(s+a)=R(s).
$$
Thus $R$ is continuous, $a$-periodic, and bounded.

For fixed $s$, put
$$
r_k=R(s+kb).
$$
The second recurrence from Step 2 gives
$$
(r_{k+1}-r_k)^2
$$
only symbolically through its characteristic polynomial; explicitly, the recurrence has characteristic polynomial
$$
(z-1)^2(z-3)^2.
$$
Therefore
$$
r_k=c_0+c_1k+(c_2+c_3k)3^k.
$$
Boundedness for all integers $k$ forces
$$
c_1=c_2=c_3=0.
$$
Hence
$$
R(s+b)=R(s).
$$

The ratio $a/b$ is irrational, since a rational relation would imply $2^m=3^n$ for positive integers $m,n$. The additive subgroup generated by $a$ and $b$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$: irrational rotations give nonzero integer combinations of $a,b$ arbitrarily close to $0$, and their integer multiples approximate every real number.

Thus the continuous function $R$ has a dense set of periods and is constant. Write
$$
R(s)=\beta a.
$$
Then
$$
Q(s)=U(s)-\beta s
$$
is $a$-periodic. A linear function is annihilated by $(E_b-1)^2$, so $Q$ still satisfies the second recurrence. For fixed $s$, the bounded sequence $Q(s+kb)$ has the same characteristic roots $1,1,3,3$, hence is constant in $k$. Thus $Q$ is also $b$-periodic and therefore constant.

Consequently,
$$
U(s)=\alpha+\beta s.
$$

Step 4: Show that the second component is exponential-affine

Write
$$
V(s)=e^sW(s).
$$
Since $e^a=2$,
$$
(E_a-2)V=2e^s(E_a-1)W.
$$
Applying this twice to $(E_a-2)^2V=0$ gives
$$
(E_a-1)^2W=0.
$$

Since $e^b=3$, conjugating the second recurrence by multiplication by $e^s$ gives
$$
(E_b-1)^2(3E_b-1)^2W=0.
$$

Set
$$
S=(E_a-1)W.
$$
Then $S$ is continuous and $a$-periodic, hence bounded. For fixed $s$, the sequence
$$
s_k=S(s+kb)
$$
has characteristic polynomial
$$
(z-1)^2(3z-1)^2.
$$
Thus
$$
s_k=d_0+d_1k+(d_2+d_3k)3^{-k}.
$$
Boundedness for all integers $k$ forces
$$
d_1=d_2=d_3=0.
$$
So $S$ has both periods $a$ and $b$, and density makes it constant.

Subtracting the corresponding linear term from $W$ leaves an $a$-periodic function satisfying the same $b$-recurrence. The same bounded-sequence argument makes that remainder $b$-periodic and therefore constant. Hence
$$
W(s)=\gamma+\delta s.
$$
Combining this with Step 3,
$$
H(s)=\alpha+\beta s+(\gamma+\delta s)e^s.
$$

Step 5: Use the four normalization conditions

From $f(e)=0$,
$$
A(1)=0,
$$
so
$$
H(0)=0.
$$
The remaining conditions give
$$
H(a)=\frac{8a}{4}=2a,
$$
$$
H(2a)=\frac{128a}{16}=8a,
$$
and
$$
H(b)=\frac{27b}{9}=3b.
$$

The function
$$
H_0(s)=se^s
$$
 has exactly these four values. Write
$$
H(s)-H_0(s)=\alpha+\beta s+(\gamma+cs)e^s,
$$
where
$$
c=\delta-1.
$$
Vanishing at $s=0$ gives
$$
\alpha=-\gamma.
$$
Vanishing at $s=a$ and $s=2a$ gives
$$
\beta a+\gamma+2ca=0,
$$
$$
2\beta a+3\gamma+8ca=0.
$$
Subtracting twice the first equation from the second yields
$$
\gamma=-4ca,
$$
and then
$$
\beta=2c,\qquad \alpha=4ca.
$$
Finally, vanishing at $s=b$ gives
$$
c(5b-8a)=0.
$$
But
$$
5b-8a=\log\frac{3^5}{2^8}\neq0
$$
because $243\neq256$. Hence
$$
c=0,
$$
and all four coefficients vanish.

Therefore
$$
H(s)=se^s.
$$
Returning to $A$,
$$
A(t)=t^2H(\log t)=t^3\log t.
$$

Step 6: Invert the nonlinear coordinate and evaluate the limit

Since
$$
P'(y)=1+4y+15y^2>0,
$$
the polynomial $P$ is strictly increasing on $\mathbb{R}$. Thus
$$
P(g(t))=t^3\log t
$$
determines $g(t)$ uniquely.

Put
$$
R=t^3\log t.
$$
As $t\to0^+$, $R\to0$. Write
$$
g=R+c_2R^2+c_3R^3+c_4R^4+c_5R^5+O(R^6).
$$
Substitution into
$$
g+2g^2+5g^3=R
$$
gives successively
$$
c_2+2=0,
$$
$$
c_3+4c_2+5=0,
$$
$$
c_4+2(c_2^2+2c_3)+15c_2=0,
$$
and
$$
c_5+4c_4+4c_2c_3+15c_3+15c_2^2=0.
$$
Therefore
$$
c_2=-2,\qquad c_3=3,\qquad c_4=10,\qquad c_5=-121.
$$
Hence
$$
f(e^t)
=
R-2R^2+3R^3+10R^4-121R^5+O(R^6).
$$

Let
$$
s=x-1.
$$
Then
$$
\frac{t}{s}=\frac{\log(1+s)}{s}\to1,
$$
and
$$
\log t-\log s=\log\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)\to0.
$$
Since $\log s\to-\infty$,
$$
\frac{\log t}{\log s}\to1.
$$
Therefore
$$
\frac{R^5}{s^{15}(\log s)^5}
=
\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)^{15}
\left(\frac{\log t}{\log s}\right)^5
\to1,
$$
while
$$
\frac{R^6}{s^{15}(\log s)^5}
=
\frac{R^5}{s^{15}(\log s)^5}R\to0.
$$
The numerator of the required limit is
$$
-121R^5+O(R^6).
$$

Final Answer: $\boxed{-121}$
