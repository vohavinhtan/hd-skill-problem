## Steps

Step 1: Extract the freedom left by the first dilation identity

Put
$$
g(t)=f(e^t),\qquad t>0.
$$
Then $g$ is continuous, $g(1)=0$, and the first identity becomes
$$
g(2t)=2g(t)+2t^2.
$$
Since
$$
(2t)^2-2t^2=2t^2,
$$
the function
$$
r(t)=g(t)-t^2
$$
satisfies
$$
r(2t)=2r(t).
$$
Define
$$
q(t)=\frac{r(t)}{t}.
$$
Then
$$
q(2t)=q(t).
$$
Hence
$$
g(t)=t^2+tq(t).
$$

The value $g(1)=0$ gives
$$
q(1)=-1.
$$

Step 2: Identify a particular solution of both dilation identities

Set
$$
g_0(t)=t^2-t.
$$
It satisfies the first identity. For the nonlinear expression in the second identity, define
$$
V(t)=g_0(t)+t g_0(t)^2.
$$
A direct expansion gives
$$
V(t)=t^5-2t^4+t^3+t^2-t.
$$
Therefore
$$
V(3t)-3V(t)
=
240t^5-156t^4+24t^3+6t^2
$$
and hence
$$
V(3t)-3V(t)=6t^2(1+4t-26t^2+40t^3).
$$
Thus $g_0$ satisfies the second identity as well.

Write
$$
q(t)=-1+h(t).
$$
Then
$$
g(t)=g_0(t)+th(t),
$$
and Step 1 gives
$$
h(2t)=h(t).
$$

Step 3: Use the nonlinear identity to obtain the second dilation invariance

For any function $y$, write
$$
\Phi_t(y)=y+t y^2.
$$
The second given identity and the identity for $g_0$ from Step 2 imply
$$
\Phi_{3t}(g(3t))-\Phi_{3t}(g_0(3t))
=
3\left(\Phi_t(g(t))-\Phi_t(g_0(t))\right).
$$

Since $g-g_0=th$, define
$$
E(t)=\Phi_t(g(t))-\Phi_t(g_0(t)).
$$
Then
$$
E(t)
=
th(t)+t\left(2g_0(t)th(t)+t^2h(t)^2\right),
$$
so
$$
\frac{E(t)}{t}
=
h(t)+2t g_0(t)h(t)+t^2h(t)^2.
$$
The relation $E(3t)=3E(t)$ therefore gives
$$
h(3t)+6t g_0(3t)h(3t)+9t^2h(3t)^2
=
h(t)+2t g_0(t)h(t)+t^2h(t)^2.
$$

Fix $t>0$ and replace $t$ by $t/2^k$. Since $h(2s)=h(s)$,
$$
h\left(\frac{t}{2^k}\right)=h(t),
\qquad
h\left(\frac{3t}{2^k}\right)=h(3t).
$$
As $k\to\infty$,
$$
\frac{t}{2^k}g_0\left(\frac{t}{2^k}\right)\to0,
\qquad
\frac{t}{2^k}g_0\left(\frac{3t}{2^k}\right)\to0,
$$
and all terms multiplied by $(t/2^k)^2$ also vanish. The displayed relation therefore reduces to
$$
h(3t)=h(t).
$$

Step 4: Eliminate the remaining continuous dilation freedom

Define
$$
H(s)=h(e^s),\qquad s\in\mathbb{R}.
$$
By Steps 1 and 3,
$$
H(s+\log2)=H(s),
\qquad
H(s+\log3)=H(s).
$$
The ratio $\log2/\log3$ is irrational, because an equality
$$
a\log2=b\log3
$$
for positive integers $a,b$ would imply $2^a=3^b$.

The additive subgroup generated by $\log2$ and $\log3$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$. Indeed, the pigeonhole principle applied to the fractional parts of
$$
0,\frac{\log2}{\log3},\ldots,\frac{M\log2}{\log3}
$$
produces nonzero subgroup elements of arbitrarily small absolute value, and integer multiples of such elements approximate every real number.

Since $H$ is continuous and has every element of this dense subgroup as a period, $H$ is constant. From
$$
q(1)=-1
$$
we have $h(1)=0$. Therefore
$$
h(t)=0
$$
for every $t>0$, so
$$
g(t)=t^2-t.
$$
Returning to $x$,
$$
f(x)=(\log x)^2-\log x.
$$

Step 5: Evaluate the fifth-order limit

Put
$$
s=x-1.
$$
Then
$$
\log(1+s)
=
s-\frac{s^2}{2}+\frac{s^3}{3}-\frac{s^4}{4}+\frac{s^5}{5}+O(s^6),
$$
and squaring gives
$$
(\log(1+s))^2
=
s^2-s^3+\frac{11}{12}s^4-\frac{5}{6}s^5+O(s^6).
$$
Subtracting the first expansion from the second,
$$
f(1+s)
=
-s+\frac{3}{2}s^2-\frac{4}{3}s^3+\frac{7}{6}s^4-\frac{31}{30}s^5+O(s^6).
$$
The terms of degrees one through four cancel with the prescribed numerator.

Final Answer: $\boxed{-\frac{31}{30}}$
