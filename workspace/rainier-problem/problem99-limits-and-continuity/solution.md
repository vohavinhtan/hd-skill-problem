## Steps

Step 1: Find a quantity with a closed dilation law

Put
$$
g(t)=f(e^t),\qquad t>0.
$$
Then $g$ is continuous and
$$
g(1)=0.
$$
The two identities become
$$
g(2t)+2t g(2t)^2
=
8g(t)+8t g(t)^2-4t^2+8t^4
$$
and
$$
g(3t)+3t g(3t)^2
=
27g(t)+27t g(t)^2-18t^2+54t^4.
$$

Define
$$
E(t)=g(t)+t g(t)^2-t^2.
$$
For the first dilation,
$$
E(2t)
=
g(2t)+2t g(2t)^2-4t^2
=
8E(t)+8t^4.
$$
For the second,
$$
E(3t)
=
g(3t)+3t g(3t)^2-9t^2
=
27E(t)+54t^4.
$$

Step 2: Remove the affine cocycle from both dilations

Since $t>0$, set
$$
B(t)=\frac{E(t)}{t}.
$$
The relations from Step 1 give
$$
B(2t)=4B(t)+4t^3,
$$
$$
B(3t)=9B(t)+18t^3.
$$
Now put
$$
C(t)=B(t)-t^3.
$$
Then
$$
C(2t)=4C(t),
$$
$$
C(3t)=9C(t).
$$
Dividing by $t^2$ gives the continuous function
$$
D(t)=\frac{C(t)}{t^2}
$$
with
$$
D(2t)=D(t),\qquad D(3t)=D(t).
$$

Step 3: Use the two dilations to determine the remaining freedom

Define
$$
Q(s)=D(e^s),\qquad s\in\mathbb{R}.
$$
Then
$$
Q(s+\log2)=Q(s),
$$
$$
Q(s+\log3)=Q(s).
$$
The ratio $\log2/\log3$ is irrational. Otherwise positive integers $a,b$ would satisfy
$$
a\log2=b\log3,
$$
which would imply
$$
2^a=3^b,
$$
contrary to unique factorization.

To see that the subgroup generated by $\log2$ and $\log3$ is dense, apply the pigeonhole principle to the fractional parts of
$$
0,\frac{\log2}{\log3},\frac{2\log2}{\log3},\ldots,\frac{M\log2}{\log3}.
$$
As $M$ grows, this produces nonzero integer combinations of $\log2$ and $\log3$ with arbitrarily small absolute value. Integer multiples of such combinations approximate every real number.

Thus $Q$ has a dense set of periods. Continuity forces $Q$ to be constant, so $D$ is constant.

At $t=1$,
$$
E(1)=g(1)+g(1)^2-1=-1.
$$
Therefore
$$
B(1)=-1,\qquad C(1)=-2,\qquad D(1)=-2.
$$
Hence
$$
D(t)=-2
$$
for every $t>0$. Reversing the definitions,
$$
C(t)=-2t^2,
$$
$$
B(t)=t^3-2t^2,
$$
and
$$
E(t)=t^4-2t^3.
$$

Step 4: Recover the correct algebraic branch of the function

From the definition of $E$,
$$
g(t)+t g(t)^2-t^2=t^4-2t^3.
$$
Thus
$$
t g(t)^2+g(t)-t^2(1-t)^2=0.
$$
The two roots are
$$
g(t)=
\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{1+4t^3(1-t)^2}}{2t}.
$$
The discriminant is strictly positive for every $t>0$, so the two root branches never meet.

At $t=1$, the two values are $0$ and $-1$. Since $g(1)=0$ and $g$ is continuous on the connected interval $(0,\infty)$, it cannot switch to the other branch. Therefore
$$
g(t)=
\frac{-1+\sqrt{1+4t^3(1-t)^2}}{2t}.
$$

Step 5: Compute the fifth-order expansion at the endpoint

As $t\to0^+$,
$$
\sqrt{1+4t^3(1-t)^2}
=
1+2t^3-4t^4+2t^5-2t^6+O(t^7).
$$
Step 4 then gives
$$
g(t)
=
t^2-2t^3+t^4-t^5+O(t^6).
$$

Put
$$
s=x-1.
$$
Since
$$
t=\log(1+s)
=
s-\frac{s^2}{2}+\frac{s^3}{3}-\frac{s^4}{4}+\frac{s^5}{5}+O(s^6),
$$
we have
$$
t^2=s^2-s^3+\frac{11}{12}s^4-\frac{5}{6}s^5+O(s^6),
$$
$$
t^3=s^3-\frac{3}{2}s^4+\frac{7}{4}s^5+O(s^6),
$$
$$
t^4=s^4-2s^5+O(s^6),
$$
and
$$
t^5=s^5+O(s^6).
$$
Substitution yields
$$
f(1+s)
=
s^2-3s^3+\frac{59}{12}s^4-\frac{22}{3}s^5+O(s^6).
$$
The terms of degrees two through four cancel with the numerator in the stated limit.

Final Answer: $\boxed{-\frac{22}{3}}$
