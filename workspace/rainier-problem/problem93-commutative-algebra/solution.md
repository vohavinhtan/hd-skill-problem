## Steps

Step 1: Identify the saturated quotient by explicit Laurent and divisor-class coordinates
Let $S=\mathbb F_p[x_v:v\in V(G_n)]$, let $J$ be the ideal generated by the vertex binomials, and let $L\subset\mathbb Z^{V(G_n)}$ be the subgroup generated by the Laplacian rows. Since
$$
x_v^{-1}=\frac{\prod_{w\neq v}x_w}{M_0},
$$
the localization $S[M_0^{-1}]$ is the Laurent ring in all vertex variables. If $\ell_v$ is the Laplacian row at $v$, then
$$
x_v^{d(v)}-\prod_{w\sim v}x_w
=\left(\prod_{w\sim v}x_w\right)(x^{\ell_v}-1),
$$
so after localization $J$ is generated by $x^{\ell_v}-1$. The identities
$$
x^{a+b}-1=x^a(x^b-1)+(x^a-1),\qquad
x^{-a}-1=-x^{-a}(x^a-1)
$$
show that the localized ideal is
$$
I_L=(x^\ell-1:\ell\in L).
$$
Also
$$
J:M_0^\infty=J S[M_0^{-1}]\cap S=I_L\cap S,
$$
because $g/1$ belongs to the localized ideal exactly when $M_0^r g\in J$ for some $r\geq0$. Hence the natural map
$$
\iota:S/(J:M_0^\infty)\longrightarrow S[M_0^{-1}]/I_L
$$
has kernel $(I_L\cap S)/(J:M_0^\infty)=0$ and is injective.

We now prove directly that the Laurent quotient has one basis vector for each coset of $L$. Let
$$
H=\mathbb Z^{V(G_n)}/L
$$
and let $\mathbb F_p[H]$ have basis $E_h$ for $h\in H$ with $E_hE_{h'}=E_{h+h'}$. Define
$$
\Psi:S[M_0^{-1}]\to\mathbb F_p[H],\qquad \Psi(x^a)=E_{[a]}.
$$
Certainly $I_L\subseteq\ker\Psi$. If $F=\sum_a c_ax^a\in\ker\Psi$, group its finitely many terms by cosets $C$ of $L$ and choose $a_C\in C$. The coefficient of $E_C$ is $\sum_{a\in C}c_a=0$, so
$$
\sum_{a\in C}c_ax^a
=\sum_{a\in C\setminus\{a_C\}}c_a(x^a-x^{a_C}).
$$
Since $a-a_C\in L$,
$$
x^a-x^{a_C}=x^{a_C}(x^{a-a_C}-1)\in I_L.
$$
Thus $\ker\Psi=I_L$, so $S[M_0^{-1}]/I_L\cong\mathbb F_p[H]$ with basis $\{E_h:h\in H\}$.

Put $R_n=S/(J:M_0^\infty)$. If monomials $x^a,x^b\in S$ satisfy $a-b\in L$, then
$$
x^a-x^b=x^b(x^{a-b}-1)\in I_L\cap S=J:M_0^\infty,
$$
so they are equal in $R_n$. Conversely, if they are equal in $R_n$, injectivity of $\iota$ gives $E_{[a]}=E_{[b]}$, hence $a-b\in L$. Any linear relation in $R_n$ among monomials from distinct cosets would inject to a relation among distinct basis vectors $E_h$, so these classes are linearly independent.

It remains to compute $H$. Put $a_0=a_n=b_0=b_n=c_0=c_n=o$. On the $a$-cycle,
$$
2[a_i]-[a_{i-1}]-[a_{i+1}]=0,
$$
so successive differences are equal and
$$
[a_i-o]=i[a_1-o],\qquad n[a_1-o]=0.
$$
The same holds on the $b$- and $c$-cycles. Define
$$
\pi(o)=(1,0,0,0),\quad
\pi(a_i)=(1,i,0,0),\quad
\pi(b_i)=(1,0,i,0),\quad
\pi(c_i)=(1,0,0,i)
$$
in $\mathbb Z\oplus(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^3$. Every Laplacian row maps to $0$; at $o$, the two neighbors on each cycle contribute $1+(n-1)=0$ modulo $n$. Hence $\pi$ induces
$$
\overline\pi:H\to\mathbb Z\oplus(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^3.
$$
Conversely send the four standard generators to $[o]$, $[a_1-o]$, $[b_1-o]$, and $[c_1-o]$. The displayed relations make this a well-defined surjection onto $H$, and composing with $\overline\pi$ gives the identity on the standard generators. Therefore
$$
H\cong\mathbb Z\oplus(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^3.
$$
Consequently the degree-$d$ part of $R_n$ has one basis vector for each degree-$d$ class with an effective representative.

Step 2: Convert the target graded piece to a finite group-algebra quotient
Let
$$
B=\mathbb F_p[(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^3]
$$
with basis $e_{x,y,z}$, where all coordinates are read modulo $n$. Since $p$ is odd, $n$ is odd, and
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}i=\frac{n(n-1)}2\equiv0\pmod n.
$$
Thus $M_0$ has class $(0,0,0)$ in the degree-zero factor. Also
$$
M_{a,j}=M_0\frac{x_{a_j}}{x_o},\qquad
M_{b,j}=M_0\frac{x_{b_j}}{x_o},\qquad
M_{c,j}=M_0\frac{x_{c_j}}{x_o}
$$
in the Laurent quotient, so these three monomials have classes $(j,0,0)$, $(0,j,0)$, and $(0,0,j)$ relative to $M_0$.

The degree of each added relation is
$$
D=|V(G_n)|=3n-2.
$$
At degree $3n=D+2$, every class of $(\mathbb Z/n)^3$ is effective: $(x,y,z)$ is represented in degree $3$ by one vertex from each cycle, using $o$ when a coordinate is $0$, and then padded with copies of $o$. Hence $(R_n)_{3n}\cong B$.

In degree $2$, the effective classes are exactly
$$
T=\{(x,y,z):xyz=0\text{ in the sense that at least one coordinate is }0\}.
$$
Indeed a sum of two vertices uses at most two of the three cycles, and conversely any class with one zero coordinate is represented by one vertex on each of the other two cycles.

Put
$$
j=\frac{(p-1)n}{p}.
$$
Therefore $(A_n)_{3n}\cong B/W$, where $W$ is spanned, for $t=(x,y,z)\in T$, by
$$
\begin{aligned}
r_1(t)&=e_{x+1,y,z}+e_{x,y+1,z}-2e_{x,y,z},\\
r_2(t)&=e_{x,y+1,z}+e_{x,y,z+1}-2e_{x,y,z},\\
r_3(t)&=e_{x+j,y,z}+e_{x,y+j,z}-2e_{x,y,z}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Step 3: Pass to the dual system and determine the support of all equations
Use the pairing for which the basis $e_{x,y,z}$ is orthonormal. Then
$$
\dim_{\mathbb F_p}(B/W)=\dim_{\mathbb F_p}W^\perp.
$$
A function $f:(\mathbb Z/n)^3\to\mathbb F_p$ belongs to $W^\perp$ exactly when, for every $(x,y,z)\in T$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x+1,y,z)+f(x,y+1,z)&=2f(x,y,z),\\
f(x,y+1,z)+f(x,y,z+1)&=2f(x,y,z),\\
f(x+j,y,z)+f(x,y+j,z)&=2f(x,y,z).
\end{aligned}
$$

Let $s=(X,Y,Z)$. For the $r_1$ family, the three possible preimages are
$$
\begin{aligned}
s=t\in T&\Longleftrightarrow X=0\text{ or }Y=0\text{ or }Z=0,\\
s=t+(1,0,0),\ t\in T&\Longleftrightarrow X=1\text{ or }Y=0\text{ or }Z=0,\\
s=t+(0,1,0),\ t\in T&\Longleftrightarrow X=0\text{ or }Y=1\text{ or }Z=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus
$$
s\in\operatorname{supp}(r_1)\Longleftrightarrow Z=0\text{ or }X\in\{0,1\}\text{ or }Y\in\{0,1\}.
$$
For the $r_2$ family,
$$
\begin{aligned}
s=t\in T&\Longleftrightarrow X=0\text{ or }Y=0\text{ or }Z=0,\\
s=t+(0,1,0),\ t\in T&\Longleftrightarrow X=0\text{ or }Y=1\text{ or }Z=0,\\
s=t+(0,0,1),\ t\in T&\Longleftrightarrow X=0\text{ or }Y=0\text{ or }Z=1,
\end{aligned}
$$
so
$$
s\in\operatorname{supp}(r_2)\Longleftrightarrow X=0\text{ or }Y\in\{0,1\}\text{ or }Z\in\{0,1\}.
$$
For the $r_3$ family,
$$
\begin{aligned}
s=t\in T&\Longleftrightarrow X=0\text{ or }Y=0\text{ or }Z=0,\\
s=t+(j,0,0),\ t\in T&\Longleftrightarrow X=j\text{ or }Y=0\text{ or }Z=0,\\
s=t+(0,j,0),\ t\in T&\Longleftrightarrow X=0\text{ or }Y=j\text{ or }Z=0,
\end{aligned}
$$
so
$$
s\in\operatorname{supp}(r_3)\Longleftrightarrow Z=0\text{ or }X\in\{0,j\}\text{ or }Y\in\{0,j\}.
$$
Each line identifies the unique preimage $t$ and tests $t\in T$, so the implications are two-sided. Taking the union of the three supports, a coordinate occurs in at least one dual equation exactly when
$$
X\in\{0,1,j\},\qquad\text{or}\qquad
Y\in\{0,1,j\},\qquad\text{or}\qquad
Z\in\{0,1\}.
$$
Since $n\geq9$ and $p$ is odd, $0,1,j$ are distinct. Therefore the untouched coordinates are exactly
$$
X\notin\{0,1,j\},\qquad
Y\notin\{0,1,j\},\qquad
Z\notin\{0,1\},
$$
and they contribute
$$
(n-3)^2(n-2)
$$
free parameters to $W^\perp$.

Step 4: Reduce all remaining equations to a two-dimensional translation system
For $f\in W^\perp$, define
$$
g(x,y)=f(x,y,0).
$$
Because $z=0$ lies in $T$, the first and third equations give
$$
g(x+1,y)+g(x,y+1)=2g(x,y),
$$
$$
g(x+j,y)+g(x,y+j)=2g(x,y).
$$
We claim that every touched value of $f$ is forced by
$$
f(x,y,z)=g(x+z,y).
$$

First set $h(y,z)=f(0,y,z)$. Subtracting the second dual equation from the first at $(0,y,z)$ gives
$$
f(1,y,z)=h(y,z+1),
$$
and the first equation then becomes
$$
h(y,z+1)+h(y+1,z)=2h(y,z).
$$
Since $h(y,0)=g(0,y)$, recursion in $z$ and the first equation for $g$ give
$$
h(y,z)=g(z,y).
$$
Hence the formula holds on the planes $x=0$ and $x=1$. The third dual equation at $(0,y,z)$ then gives
$$
f(j,y,z)=2g(z,y)-g(z,y+j)=g(z+j,y),
$$
using the second equation for $g$.

Next, on the plane $y=0$, subtraction of the first two dual equations gives
$$
f(x,0,z+1)=f(x+1,0,z),
$$
so $f(x,0,z)=g(x+z,0)$. The first and third equations at $(x,0,z)$ then give the same formula on $y=1$ and $y=j$. The cases $z=0$ and $z=1$ follow directly from the definition of $g$ and the subtraction identity at $z=0$. These planes are exactly the touched set found in Step 3.

Conversely, if $g$ satisfies the two displayed translation equations, defining $f(x,y,z)=g(x+z,y)$ on the touched set makes all three dual equations hold, while the untouched coordinates remain arbitrary. Therefore
$$
\dim W^\perp=(n-3)^2(n-2)+\dim\mathcal G,
$$
where $\mathcal G$ is the space of functions $g:(\mathbb Z/n)^2\to\mathbb F_p$ satisfying the two translation equations.

Step 5: Compute the residual dimension using the positive-characteristic nilpotent block
Let
$$
C=\mathbb F_p[u,v]/(u^n-1,v^n-1).
$$
Under the coefficient pairing on the basis $u^xv^y$, the orthogonal complement of the span of all translates of
$$
u+v-2,\qquad u^j+v^j-2
$$
is exactly $\mathcal G$. Hence
$$
\dim\mathcal G
=
\dim_{\mathbb F_p}
\frac{C}{(u+v-2,\ u^j+v^j-2)}.
$$

Write $q=n/p$, so $j=(p-1)q$, and set $X=u-1$. The first relation gives $v=1-X$. Since $n$ is a power of $p$,
$$
u^n-1=(u-1)^n=X^n,
$$
and the relation $v^n-1$ gives the same nilpotence condition. Thus the residual quotient is
$$
\frac{\mathbb F_p[X]}
{\left(X^n,\ (1+X)^j+(1-X)^j-2\right)}.
$$
Because $q$ is also a power of $p$,
$$
(1+X)^q=1+X^q,\qquad
(1-X)^q=1-X^q.
$$
With $Y=X^q$,
$$
(1+X)^j+(1-X)^j-2
=
(1+Y)^{p-1}+(1-Y)^{p-1}-2.
$$
The constant term is $0$, all odd powers cancel, and the coefficient of $Y^2$ is
$$
2\binom{p-1}{2}\equiv2\pmod p,
$$
which is nonzero because $p$ is odd. Therefore
$$
(1+Y)^{p-1}+(1-Y)^{p-1}-2
=
Y^2U(Y)
$$
with $U(0)=2$, so $U(Y)$ is a unit in the truncated ring. Since $2q<n$, the residual quotient is isomorphic as a vector space to
$$
\mathbb F_p[X]/(X^{2q}),
$$
and consequently
$$
\dim\mathcal G=2q=\frac{2n}{p}.
$$
Combining this with Step 4 yields
$$
\dim_{\mathbb F_p}(A_n)_{3n}
=
(n-3)^2(n-2)+\frac{2n}{p}.
$$
Final Answer: $\boxed{(n-3)^2(n-2)+\frac{2n}{p}}$
