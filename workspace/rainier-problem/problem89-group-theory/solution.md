## Steps

Step 1: Classify the linear rank-isometries of $V$
The trace pairing is nondegenerate. Indeed, the absolute trace is not the zero map because the nonzero polynomial
$$
T+T^2+\cdots+T^{2^{r-1}}
$$
has degree below $2^r$ and therefore cannot vanish on all of $E$. If $x\ne0$, choose $z$ with $\operatorname{Tr}(z)=1$ and take $y=x^{-1}z$.

Every rank-one endomorphism has the form
$$
R_{u,v}(x)=u\operatorname{Tr}(vx).
$$
For fixed nonzero $u$ or $v$, the spaces $\{R_{u,w}:w\in E\}$ and $\{R_{w,v}:w\in E\}$ are the two families of maximal subspaces whose nonzero elements have rank $1$. To see maximality, if $R_{u,v}+R_{u',v'}$ has rank at most $1$ and $u,u'$ are independent, then $v,v'$ must be dependent; applying this to every pair gives one of the two displayed families.

Hence an invertible linear rank-preserver either preserves these two families or swaps them. Intersections of coordinate members of the two families are the one-dimensional matrix-unit spaces, so their images give two bases and force
$$
L(X)=AXB
\quad\text{or}\quad
L(X)=AX^\dagger B
$$
with $A,B\in\operatorname{GL}_{\mathbb F_2}(E)$. Conversely both forms preserve rank. Thus every element of $G$ is uniquely a translation followed by one of these linear maps.

Step 2: Determine the linear stabilizer of $\mathcal C$
Let
$$
\mathcal L=\{M_a:a\in E\}.
$$
The left and right idealizers of $\mathcal C$ are both $\mathcal L$. For the left idealizer, if $P\mathcal C\subseteq\mathcal C$, then $I\in\mathcal C$ gives $P=M_u+M_vF$. Since $F\in\mathcal C$,
$$
PF=M_uF+M_vF^2\in\mathcal C,
$$
so $v=0$. Indeed, a relation among $I,F,F^2$ would give a polynomial of degree at most $4<2^r$ vanishing on all of $E$. Conversely every $M_u$ sends $\mathcal C$ into itself. The right idealizer is identical, using $FP$ instead.

If $\mathcal I_\ell,\mathcal I_r$ denote the two idealizers, then
$$
\mathcal I_\ell(A\mathcal CB)=A\mathcal I_\ell(\mathcal C)A^{-1},
\qquad
\mathcal I_r(A\mathcal CB)=B^{-1}\mathcal I_r(\mathcal C)B.
$$
Thus a map $X\mapsto AXB$ stabilizing $\mathcal C$ forces both $A$ and $B$ to normalize $\mathcal L$. The adjoint code $\mathcal C^\dagger$ has the same two idealizers $\mathcal L$, so the same conclusion holds for $X\mapsto AX^\dagger B$.

Choose a primitive $\alpha\in E^\times$. If $A$ normalizes $\mathcal L$, then
$$
AM_\alpha A^{-1}=M_{\alpha^k}
$$
for some $k$. Writing $u=A(1)$ gives $A(p(\alpha))=p(\alpha^k)u$. Since the two multiplication maps are conjugate, $\alpha^k$ has the same minimal polynomial as $\alpha$, hence $\alpha^k=\alpha^{2^i}$ for a unique $0\leq i<r$. Thus
$$
A=M_uF^i.
$$
The same holds for $B$.

For $X=M_aF^j$,
$$
(M_uF^i)X(M_vF^s)
$$
has Frobenius exponent $i+j+s$. Since $\mathcal C$ has exactly the exponents $0,1$ and $r\geq3$, a direct-type stabilizer must have $s=-i$. For the adjoint type, $(M_aF^j)^\dagger$ has exponent $-j$, so preservation of $\{0,1\}$ forces $s=1-i$. Hence the linear stabilizer of $\mathcal C$ consists exactly of
$$
X\mapsto M_uF^iXM_vF^{-i}
$$
and
$$
X\mapsto M_uF^iX^\dagger M_vF^{1-i},
$$
where $u,v\in E^\times$ and $0\leq i<r$.

Step 3: Find the linear normalizer of the cyclic symmetry generated by $J$
Put
$$
\sigma(X)=FXF^{-1}.
$$
Since $F^\dagger=F^{-1}$,
$$
J^2(X)=(X^\dagger F)^\dagger F=F^{-1}XF=\sigma^{-1}(X).
$$
Also $J$ commutes with $\sigma$. The map $J$ swaps the two rank-one families whereas every power of $\sigma$ preserves them, so $J\notin\langle\sigma\rangle$. Therefore
$$
|\langle J\rangle|=2r,
\qquad
\langle J^2\rangle=\langle\sigma\rangle.
$$

Consider a direct stabilizer from Step 2 with $A=M_uF^i$ and $B=M_vF^{-i}$. If it normalizes $\langle\sigma\rangle$, then its conjugate of $\sigma$ is $\sigma^k$ for some $k$. The left factor of that conjugate is
$$
AFA^{-1}=M_{u^{-1}}F.
$$
Thus $k=1$ and $u=1$; the right factor similarly gives $v=1$. Hence the direct normalizers of $\langle\sigma\rangle$ are precisely the powers of $\sigma$.

Now $J$ itself commutes with $\sigma$. If an adjoint-type stabilizer normalizes $\langle\sigma\rangle$, multiplying it by $J^{-1}$ gives a direct-type normalizer, hence it is $\sigma^iJ$. Therefore
$$
N_{\operatorname{Stab}(\mathcal C)}(\langle\sigma\rangle)=\langle J\rangle.
$$
Since $\langle J^2\rangle$ is the subgroup of squares of the cyclic group $\langle J\rangle$, it is characteristic there. Any element normalizing $\langle J\rangle$ must therefore normalize $\langle\sigma\rangle$, so
$$
N_{\operatorname{Stab}(\mathcal C)}(\langle J\rangle)=\langle J\rangle.
$$
Thus there are exactly $2r$ admissible linear parts for a normalizer element of $H$.

Step 4: Reduce the affine part to a fixed-space problem
The full translation subgroup of $G$ is normal, and
$$
H\cap\{\text{translations}\}=\{\tau_C:C\in\mathcal C\}.
$$
Hence a normalizer element $g(X)=L(X)+D$ must satisfy $L(\mathcal C)=\mathcal C$. Its induced action on the quotient by these translations must normalize $\langle J\rangle$, so Step 3 gives $L\in\langle J\rangle$. Such an $L$ already lies in $H$, so it remains only to decide when $\tau_D$ normalizes $H$.

Translations commute with every $\tau_C$, while
$$
\tau_DJ\tau_D^{-1}=\tau_{D+J(D)}J.
$$
Therefore
$$
\tau_D\in N_G(H)
\iff
D+J(D)\in\mathcal C.
$$

Every endomorphism has a unique expansion
$$
D=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}M_{d_j}F^j.
$$
Uniqueness follows because a nonzero linearized polynomial of degree at most $2^{r-1}$ cannot vanish at all $2^r$ elements of $E$. Put
$$
V_j=\{M_aF^j:a\in E\}.
$$
Then
$$
V=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{r-1}V_j,
\qquad
\mathcal C=V_0\oplus V_1.
$$
A direct adjoint calculation gives
$$
J(M_aF^j)=M_{a^{2^{r-j}}}F^{1-j},
$$
with the exponent read modulo $r$. Thus both $\mathcal C$ and
$$
W=\bigoplus_{j=2}^{r-1}V_j
$$
are $J$-invariant, and $D+J(D)\in\mathcal C$ exactly when the $W$-component of $D$ is fixed by $J$.

Step 5: Count the fixed space and finish
If $w\in W$ is fixed by $J$, then it is fixed by
$$
J^2=\sigma^{-1}.
$$
On each $V_j$, the map $\sigma$ sends $M_aF^j$ to $M_{a^2}F^j$. Hence every coefficient of a $J$-fixed vector in $W$ lies in $\mathbb F_2$. On these binary coefficients, $J$ simply permutes the basis components by
$$
j\longmapsto1-j\pmod r
$$
on the index set $\{2,\ldots,r-1\}$.

This involution has no fixed index when $r$ is even and one fixed index when $r$ is odd. Consequently its number of orbits is
$$
\left\lfloor\frac{r-1}{2}\right\rfloor.
$$
Thus
$$
|\operatorname{Fix}_W(J)|
=
2^{\left\lfloor\frac{r-1}{2}\right\rfloor}.
$$
The component in $\mathcal C$ is arbitrary and $|\mathcal C|=2^{2r}$, so the number of admissible translations is
$$
2^{2r+\left\lfloor\frac{r-1}{2}\right\rfloor}.
$$
Multiplying by the $2r$ linear parts from Step 3 gives
$$
|N_G(H)|
=
r\,2^{2r+1+\left\lfloor\frac{r-1}{2}\right\rfloor}.
$$
Final Answer: $\boxed{r\,2^{2r+1+\left\lfloor\frac{r-1}{2}\right\rfloor}}$
