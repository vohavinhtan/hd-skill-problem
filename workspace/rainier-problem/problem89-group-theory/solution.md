## Steps

Step 1: Classify the linear rank-isometries of $V$
Let $\operatorname{Tr}:E\to\mathbb F_2$ be the absolute trace. The polynomial
$$
T+T^2+\cdots+T^{2^{r-1}}
$$
is nonzero and has degree below $|E|$, so it cannot vanish on every element of $E$. Thus $\operatorname{Tr}$ is nonzero. The pairing $(x,y)\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}(xy)$ is then nondegenerate: for $x\ne0$, choose $z$ with $\operatorname{Tr}(z)=1$ and take $y=x^{-1}z$. Let $X^\dagger$ denote the adjoint for this pairing.

Every rank-one endomorphism has the form
$$
R_{u,v}(x)=u\operatorname{Tr}(vx).
$$
For fixed nonzero $u$ or $v$, the spaces $\{R_{u,w}:w\in E\}$ and $\{R_{w,v}:w\in E\}$ have dimension $r$ and every nonzero element has rank $1$. They are maximal with this property. Indeed, if a rank-one subspace contains $R_{u,v}$ and $R_{u',v'}$ with $u,u'$ independent, then $v,v'$ must be dependent; otherwise the sum has two independent image directions and two independent coordinate functionals, hence rank $2$. Thus $v'=v$. Any third element must also have second factor $v$, because an element with first factor $u$ but second factor different from $v$ would have rank-$2$ sum with $R_{u',v}$. The alternative case has all first factors equal.

Hence an invertible linear rank-preserver either preserves these two families of maximal rank-one subspaces or swaps them. Using intersections of coordinate members of the two families gives
$$
L(X)=AXB
\quad\text{or}\quad
L(X)=AX^\dagger B
$$
with $A,B\in\operatorname{GL}_{\mathbb F_2}(E)$. Conversely both forms preserve rank. Thus every $g\in G$ is uniquely a translation followed by one of these linear maps.

Step 2: Extract the linear subgroup and the distinguished translation
Write
$$
\rho(X)=M_\alpha X M_{\alpha^{-1}},\qquad
\sigma(X)=FXF^{-1},\qquad
t(X)=X+I,
$$
so $H=\langle\rho,\sigma,t\rangle$. Both $\rho$ and $\sigma$ fix $I$, so $t$ commutes with them. If $K=\langle\rho,\sigma\rangle$, then every element of $H$ is uniquely $t^\epsilon k$ with $\epsilon\in\mathbb F_2$ and $k\in K$. Therefore the intersection of $H$ with the full translation subgroup of $G$ is exactly $\{1,t\}$.

If $g(X)=L(X)+C$ normalizes $H$, conjugation by $g$ sends a translation by $D$ to a translation by $L(D)$. Hence it must fix the unique nonidentity translation in $H$, so
$$
L(I)=I.
$$
For the two forms from Step 1 this forces $B=A^{-1}$. Thus the linear part of any normalizer element is
$$
X\mapsto AXA^{-1}
\quad\text{or}\quad
X\mapsto AX^\dagger A^{-1}.
$$
Moreover its conjugation action must normalize $K$.

Step 3: Determine the inner linear normalizer from the derived subgroup
Let
$$
S=\langle M_\alpha,F\rangle\leq\operatorname{GL}_{\mathbb F_2}(E).
$$
The map $U\mapsto(X\mapsto UXU^{-1})$ is injective because the center of $\operatorname{GL}_{\mathbb F_2}(E)$ is $\mathbb F_2^\times I=\{I\}$, and its image is $K$.

Since $FM_aF^{-1}=M_{a^2}$,
$$
FM_aF^{-1}M_a^{-1}=M_a
$$
for every $a\in E^\times$. Hence $S'$ contains all multiplication maps $M_a$. Conversely $S/\{M_a:a\in E^\times\}$ is generated by the image of $F$ and is cyclic, so
$$
S'=\{M_a:a\in E^\times\}.
$$
Thus any $A$ normalizing $S$ also normalizes this cyclic multiplication subgroup.

Suppose $A$ normalizes $\{M_a:a\in E^\times\}$. Then
$$
AM_\alpha A^{-1}=M_{\alpha^k}
$$
for some $k$ coprime to $2^r-1$. Put $a=A(1)$. From $A(\alpha x)=\alpha^kA(x)$ we obtain
$$
A(p(\alpha))=p(\alpha^k)a
$$
for every $p\in\mathbb F_2[T]$. Since $M_\alpha$ and $M_{\alpha^k}$ are conjugate, $\alpha^k$ has the same minimal polynomial as $\alpha$, so
$$
\alpha^k=\alpha^{2^j}
$$
for a unique $0\leq j<r$. Therefore
$$
A(x)=a x^{2^j}=M_aF^j(x).
$$
Hence the normalizer of the multiplication subgroup is exactly $S$, and consequently $N_{\operatorname{GL}_{\mathbb F_2}(E)}(S)=S$. Since $|S|=r(2^r-1)$, there are exactly $r(2^r-1)$ inner linear normalizers of $K$.

Step 4: Include the adjoint branch
Let $\Delta(X)=X^\dagger$. Multiplication maps are self-adjoint because
$$
\operatorname{Tr}(axy)=\operatorname{Tr}(xay).
$$
Also $F^\dagger=F^{-1}$, since
$$
\operatorname{Tr}(x^2y)=\operatorname{Tr}(xy^{2^{r-1}}).
$$
Therefore
$$
\Delta\rho\Delta=\rho^{-1},
\qquad
\Delta\sigma\Delta=\sigma.
$$
Thus $\Delta$ normalizes $K$. Every ruling-swapping linear normalizer is an inner one followed by $\Delta$, and the two types are disjoint because one preserves the two rank-one families while the other swaps them. Hence the total number of admissible linear parts is
$$
2r(2^r-1).
$$

Step 5: Determine the admissible affine translation parts
Fix an admissible linear part $L$ and write $g(X)=L(X)+C$. Since $L$ normalizes $K$, the condition $gHg^{-1}=H$ is equivalent to
$$
C+k(C)\in\{0,I\}
\qquad(k\in K).
$$
For $k=\rho^i$, write
$$
C+\rho^i(C)=\epsilon_i I,
\qquad \epsilon_i\in\mathbb F_2.
$$
Because every $\rho^i$ fixes $I$, the rule $\rho^i\mapsto\epsilon_i$ is a homomorphism from the odd-order group $\langle\rho\rangle$ to the group of order $2$. It is therefore trivial. Thus $C$ commutes with $M_\alpha$, and since $\alpha$ generates $E$ over $\mathbb F_2$,
$$
C=M_c
$$
for a unique $c\in E$.

It remains to impose the generator $\sigma$. Since $FM_cF^{-1}=M_{c^2}$,
$$
C+\sigma(C)\in\{0,I\}
\iff
c^2+c\in\mathbb F_2.
$$
The map $c\mapsto c^2+c$ is $\mathbb F_2$-linear with kernel $\mathbb F_2$, so its image has size $2^{r-1}$. Its image lies in the trace-zero hyperplane because $\operatorname{Tr}(c^2+c)=0$; by Step 1 that hyperplane also has size $2^{r-1}$, so the two are equal. Thus $c^2+c=0$ always has two solutions. The equation $c^2+c=1$ has two solutions exactly when $\operatorname{Tr}(1)=0$, which occurs exactly when $r$ is even. Consequently the number of admissible $C$ is
$$
2^{\gcd(2,r)}.
$$

Step 6: Multiply the independent choices
Step 4 gives $2r(2^r-1)$ admissible linear parts, and Step 5 gives $2^{\gcd(2,r)}$ admissible translations for each one. Therefore
$$
|N_G(H)|
=2^{\gcd(2,r)}\cdot2r(2^r-1)
=2^{1+\gcd(2,r)}r(2^r-1).
$$
Final Answer: $\boxed{2^{1+\gcd(2,r)}r(2^r-1)}$
